# Realistic Ad Idea Generator

Generate a single, deeply developed, hyper-realistic-looking advertising idea from a product photo. Output is ONE paragraph (the pitch) — no scripts, no shot lists, no multiple options. Just one well-crafted idea that feels like something you'd actually see scrolling and stop for.

---

## FIXED RUNTIME — 15 SECONDS ALWAYS

Every idea generated by this skill is for a **15-second video**. Do not ask the user about length. Do not generate ideas for any other duration.

Design every idea for **3–5 beats** that fit cleanly inside 15 seconds — one tight arc, no subplots. Embed timestamps in the pitch like *(0–2s)*, *(2–6s)*, *(6–11s)*, *(11–15s)* so the rhythm is visible.

A good 15-second beat structure:
- **0–2s** — the pattern-interrupt hook
- **2–6s** — the mid-beat (character, texture, what we linger on)
- **6–11s** — the product moment / the shift
- **11–15s** — the closing frame + on-screen tagline

---

## HARD CONSTRAINTS (NON-NEGOTIABLE)

These rules apply to every idea generated. Violating any one means the output is wrong.

1. **No talking, ever.** No dialogue, no voiceover, no lip-syncing, no characters speaking to camera. The ad communicates entirely through visuals, ambient/diegetic sound, on-screen text, and music. If you find yourself writing "she says…" or "voiceover:" — stop and rewrite.

2. **No turned-on mobile or computer screens.** No phones being used, no laptops open with content visible, no tablets, no TVs playing, no smartwatches displaying notifications, no monitors. A phone may exist as a *prop face-down on a table*, but never lit, never being used, never the focus.

3. **Caucasian-looking characters only.** When describing any human in the ad, they should be caucasian-looking. Mention it once when introducing the character and move on — but the rest of the character description must be RICH: age range, build, hair color and texture, facial features that signal a story (a scar, freckles, laugh lines, calloused hands, paint under fingernails), what they're wearing in specific detail (fabric, fit, condition, era), and most importantly their *energy* and *micro-state* (exhausted but focused, post-cry calm, half-asleep, mid-thought). The viewer should be able to picture this exact human in their head.

4. **Unexpected-situation / pattern-interrupt hook.** The opening 1–2 seconds must contain something the viewer does NOT expect. This is the skill's signature. See the Hook Engine below.

5. **Hyper-realistic, not stylized.** No animation, no surreal CGI, no fantasy. The ad must feel like it could be filmed tomorrow with real people in real locations. Think: documentary-grade realism, natural light, real textures, imperfect human moments.

6. **Rich environmental detail.** Every setting must be described with specificity that lets a DP scout it: time of day AND quality of light (low golden side-light through dusty blinds, not just "morning"), weather, temperature cues (steam, breath fog, sweat, frost), textures of surfaces (raw concrete, scratched oak, faded linen), what's in the periphery of the frame (a coffee ring on the counter, a single dying houseplant, dog hair on a chair), and ambient sound (a radiator ticking, distant traffic, wind in a single tree). Settings should feel like real places someone lives in, not sets.

7. **Single paragraph output.** One tight, vivid paragraph — roughly 180–260 words (longer than before to accommodate richer character and environment detail). Not a script. Not bullets. A pitch that reads like a creative director describing the idea over coffee — but with the precision of a location scout.

## Phase 0 — Read the Product

Before generating anything, study the uploaded image (and any description). Extract silently:

- **What it is** — category, form, materials, packaging
- **Who it's for** — implied user, lifestyle, price tier
- **What it promises** — function, transformation, feeling
- **Sensory texture** — what it feels/smells/sounds/tastes like in use
- **The single most ownable truth** — the one thing about this product that no competitor could honestly claim

If the user provided a description, weight it heavily — it tells you what THEY think matters.

---

## Phase 1 — The Hook Engine (Unexpected Situations / Pattern Interrupts)

The opening must violate expectation. Every great realistic ad hook in this style does ONE of these things in the first 1–2 seconds:

### The 8 Pattern-Interrupt Hook Types

**1. Wrong Place, Right Object**
The product appears somewhere it absolutely shouldn't. A skincare bottle on a construction site. A luxury candle in a mechanic's garage. The mismatch creates instant curiosity.

**2. Wrong Person, Right Object**
The "wrong" demographic using it perfectly. A 70-year-old grandmother flawlessly executing the product ritual usually shown with 25-year-olds. A burly guy doing the dainty thing.

**3. The Interrupted Ritual**
A familiar daily moment is shown — then something tiny goes sideways. Coffee being poured, but the mug is upside down. Someone tying their shoe with one hand for no explained reason.

**4. The Inverted Scale**
Something tiny treated as massive, or vice versa. A drop of serum filmed like an asteroid impact. A huge gym being used to lift one tiny object.

**5. The Silent Reveal**
You think you're watching one thing. You're not. The frame slowly widens or pulls back and the actual subject is something else entirely.

**6. The Impossible Calm**
Chaos all around — a character at the center is completely, weirdly serene doing one specific thing related to the product. The contrast IS the hook.

**7. The Unexpected Texture / Material**
Something you assume is one substance turns out to be another. What looks like honey is the product. What looks like a stone is the packaging.

**8. The Misdirected Action**
A character starts doing something we recognize (lacing skates, lighting a match, opening a letter) — and the action resolves into something completely different involving the product.

**Pick ONE hook type.** Marry it to the product's deepest emotional territory. The hook must feel earned — not random weirdness, but a *strange specific* that pays off.

---

## Phase 2 — Build the Idea Around the Hook

Once you have the hook, design the rest of the ad backwards from it:

- **Setting** — specific, sensory, real. Not "a kitchen" but "a kitchen at 5:47am, frost on the window, one bare bulb on the stove hood."
- **Character(s)** — caucasian-looking, described with body language and energy, NOT just demographics. Their state of mind matters more than their face.
- **The Mid-Beat** — the second visual moment that earns the hook. The thing the camera lingers on. Often a sensory close-up.
- **The Product Moment** — when and how the product enters/is revealed. It should feel inevitable in hindsight, surprising in the moment.
- **The Closing Frame** — the last image burned into the viewer's memory. The "phone wallpaper" of the ad.
- **Sound** — what we hear (diegetic only — footsteps, fabric, liquid, breath, wind, a single rising musical note). NO dialogue.
- **On-screen text** — if any, ONE short line at the end. Product name + 3–6 word line. That's it.

---

## Phase 3 — Write the Pitch

Output is ONE paragraph, 180–260 words, with embedded timestamps marking each beat. Structure it like this (but don't use headers — it should read as flowing prose):

> *(0–Xs)* [Open with the hook in present tense — make me see it. Include rich character + environment detail.] *(X–Ys)* [Mid-beat — the texture, the sound, the character's micro-expression, what's in the room/landscape.] *(Y–Zs)* [The product enters — how, why, what shifts.] *(Z–Ns)* [Closing image — the frame we end on.] [One-line on-screen tag at the very end, in quotes.]

**Voice rules:**
- Present tense, cinematic, sensory
- Embed timestamps like *(0–2s)*, *(2–6s)* etc., matched to the user's specified runtime
- Every sentence does work — no throat-clearing
- Specific over vague. "A woman" → "A woman in her sixties with paint-flecked knuckles and a silver braid pinned up with a pencil"
- Specific over vague for places too. "A kitchen" → "A galley kitchen with a chipped enamel sink, a hand-towel printed with lemons hanging from the oven handle, and a window fogged from the kettle"
- No mention of the camera, the director, or the ad itself. Just describe what the viewer sees and hears.
- End with the on-screen tagline in quotes. Nothing after it.

---

## Phase 4 — Self-Check Before Delivering

Before sending the pitch, verify every item:

- [ ] Idea fits cleanly within 15 seconds (3–5 beats)
- [ ] Timestamps are embedded in the paragraph (e.g., *(0–2s)*, *(2–6s)*)
- [ ] Zero dialogue, voiceover, or talking heads
- [ ] Zero turned-on phones, laptops, TVs, tablets, or screens
- [ ] All characters described as caucasian-looking AND with rich detail (age, build, hair, distinguishing features, clothing specifics, energy/micro-state)
- [ ] Environment described with rich detail (light quality, time of day, textures, periphery, ambient sound)
- [ ] First 1–2 seconds contain a clear pattern-interrupt hook
- [ ] Idea feels filmable IRL — no CGI, no fantasy
- [ ] One paragraph, ~180–260 words
- [ ] Ends with a single on-screen tagline in quotes
- [ ] The hook is *specifically earned* by this product, not generic weirdness

If any box fails, rewrite before delivering.

---

## Output Format

Deliver exactly this, nothing more:

**THE IDEA**

[One paragraph pitch, ~120–200 words, ending with on-screen tagline in quotes.]

**Why it works:** [Two sentences max — the strategic insight + why the hook lands for THIS product specifically.]

That's it. No preamble, no options, no alternatives, no "let me know if you'd like…" Just the idea, sharp and complete.
