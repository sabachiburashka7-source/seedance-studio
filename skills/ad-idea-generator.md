# Organic TikTok Ad Idea Generator

Generate a single, ultra-realistic, organic-feeling TikTok ad idea from a product photo. The idea is psychologically engineered using Chase Hughes' **FATE model** (Focus, Authority, Tribe, Emotion) — but the FATE scaffolding is INVISIBLE in the final output. The user just sees one paragraph that, when generated by an AI video tool, looks indistinguishable from a real person's organic post.

Output is ONE paragraph. No options. No alternatives. No FATE labels in the result.

---

## FIXED RUNTIME — 15 SECONDS ALWAYS

Every idea is for a **15-second TikTok-style video**. Do not ask the user about length. Design every idea around **3–4 beats** that fit cleanly inside 15 seconds.

Embed timestamps in the paragraph like *(0–2s)*, *(2–6s)*, *(6–11s)*, *(11–15s)* so the rhythm is visible to whoever feeds this into the video generator.

A good 15-second TikTok beat structure:
- **0–2s** → the Focus hook (novelty / pattern-interrupt that stops the scroll)
- **2–6s** → the Authority + Tribe build (credibility cue + "people like me" signal)
- **6–11s** → the product moment (the shift, the proof, the texture)
- **11–15s** → the Emotion landing (the feeling you walk away with) — a quiet final image, NO text overlay

---

## THE CORE PRINCIPLE — INVISIBLE ENGINEERING

The whole point of this skill is that the ad **must not feel like an ad**. The FATE model is the load-bearing structure underneath — but if the viewer can see the structure, it has failed. Every line of the output paragraph should read like a slice of someone's real day that happens to feature the product. Engineered psychology, organic surface.

The four FATE levers map onto TikTok content like this (use them silently — never label them in the output):

### F — FOCUS (the scroll-stopper) — THIS IS THE MOST IMPORTANT PILLAR
*Novelty = focus.* The first 1–2 seconds must produce an involuntary "wait WHAT" — a visceral, physical, pattern-breaking visual that stops the thumb BEFORE the brain processes what's happening. Soft hooks kill the ad. The hook must be *visually loud* even if the scene is quiet.

**The hook test:** If you described only the first second of the ad to a stranger and they wouldn't say "wait, what?" — the hook is too weak. Rewrite it.

**Hooks that FAIL (do NOT use):**
- Someone pausing to think
- Someone looking at sunset / out a window
- Someone unfolding clothes / opening a box calmly
- Someone sitting and sighing
- A pretty product on a pretty surface
- "Mid-action" that's just normal life (brushing teeth, pouring coffee, scrolling)
- Anything that could open any lifestyle vlog

**Hooks that WORK (the actual viral hook patterns of 2025–26):**

1. **PHYSICAL ACT OF CHAOS** — something is *spilling, breaking, falling, ripping, dripping, smoking, leaking, splashing, shattering, melting, snapping, popping, exploding* in the first frame. The product is involved or adjacent. Real example energy: a tower of dishes about to collapse / someone catching a falling glass mid-air / something boiling over on a stove / a bag bursting / lipstick smashed into carpet.

2. **WRONG-BODY HOOK** — the human is doing something with their body that creates an immediate question. Eating something weird. Crying while laughing. Holding something against their face. Lying on the floor of a hallway. Sitting in the bathtub fully clothed. Eating cereal in the shower. The body position itself is the hook.

3. **EXTREME CLOSE-UP COLD OPEN** — open on a macro shot so tight the viewer can't tell what they're looking at for a half-second. A pore. A bead of liquid. The inside of a mouth. A pupil dilating. The texture of skin/fabric/food filling the entire frame, then pull back to reveal context.

4. **THE BLURT** — a single uncensored sentence said aloud in the first second, often to no one, often slightly unhinged. "I think i'm gonna throw up." "He just texted me." "This is my villain origin story." "Don't tell my mom." Said flat, not performed. Then the visual answers what the line means.

5. **THE BEFORE/AFTER IN FIRST SECOND** — open on the chaos, the aftermath, the destruction, the mess, the worst-version-of-the-moment FIRST. Mascara streaked down a face. A sink full of dishes. A bedroom destroyed. A bathroom counter covered in failed products. The hook is the wreckage; the product becomes the turn.

6. **PHYSICAL TRANSFORMATION MID-FRAME** — something is visibly changing color, texture, shape, state. Foam rising, ice melting, hair drying, skin reacting, paint dripping, makeup smearing under tears, dough rising in fast motion, a product changing color when touched.

7. **THE FOURTH-WALL VIOLATION** — they look directly at the lens with an unreadable expression for a beat too long before anything happens. No talking. Just eye contact that creates "what is she about to do." Then they break it and do the thing.

8. **THE WHISPER-CONFESSION** — a single short line muttered out loud (not to camera, to themselves or under their breath) that lands like an unfiltered confession in the first second. "i can't believe i'm doing this again." "okay don't tell anyone." "day three." "my mom would kill me." "i look insane right now." Said flat, half to self, while the visual stays mundane on purpose. The voice is the hook; the visual builds intrigue around it. (This replaces the "on-screen text claim" hook that would normally exist — text is banned in this skill because AI video generators render it badly and it kills organic feel.)

9. **PHYSICAL DISCOMFORT / BODY HORROR (mild)** — visible sweat, a stress rash, a popped pimple about to be addressed, dry cracked hands, a sunburn line, smudged makeup, a hangover face. Bodies-being-bodies hooks. The viewer recognizes the state instantly.

10. **THE INTERRUPTED MOMENT** — they're in the middle of something *intimate or chaotic* when the camera "catches" them. Crying in their car. Eating in their closet. Talking to themselves in the mirror. Mid-argument with no one. The viewer feels like they walked in on something.

**Hook-marriage rule:** The hook must connect to the product's emotional territory, but the connection should only become clear in beat 3 or 4. A spilled drink hooks because it's chaos; a spilled drink that becomes "okay so now I'm trying [product] which doesn't stain" earns the hook. Without the payoff connection, the hook is just random — with it, the whole video clicks.

The product is NEVER the hook. The anomaly is the hook.

### A — AUTHORITY (the trust cue, disguised)
On TikTok, authority is never "as a dermatologist..." said to camera. It's **shown**, not stated. It leaks through environmental detail: the right tools in the background, hands that look like they know what they're doing, a workspace that reads as expertise, a casual reference to having done this many times. Authority on TikTok is the *worn-in* feeling — someone who clearly lives this, not someone performing it. Show the wear, the routine, the muscle memory.

### T — TRIBE (the "she's like me" signal)
This is the most important lever for organic feel. The character must read as *one specific person from a specific tribe* — not a generic actor. Tribes on TikTok are micro-identities: the burnt-out grad student, the new mom in the 4am haze, the contractor in his truck, the 60-year-old learning Pilates, the gym bro who reads, the bartender after close. Pick a specific tribe and load the frame with their signals: the specific clothing, the specific space, the specific objects in the background, the specific tiredness or aliveness on their face. The viewer should be able to think "oh she's like ___" in under 2 seconds.

### E — EMOTION (the closing feeling)
The ad does not end on the product. It ends on a *feeling*. The product is the cause; the closing frame is the emotional aftermath. Quiet relief. A small private smile. A breath finally let out. A laugh caught off-guard. Something *softened*. The viewer's last impression should be an emotional state, not a CTA. The final beat is purely visual — a held shot of the emotional aftermath. NO text overlay, NO caption, NO subtitle. If a closing audible line is needed, it's one short muttered sentence (3–8 words) said by the character, not narration to the camera.

## HARD CONSTRAINTS (NON-NEGOTIABLE)

These rules apply to every idea generated:

1. **Looks organic, not produced.** No tripod-perfect framing, no studio lighting, no agency aesthetics. Phone-shot energy: slightly handheld, available light, real rooms with real clutter, normal exposure. The footage should look like it could have been posted by anyone.

2. **No "speaking-to-camera" monologue ads.** A character can be talking, laughing, sighing, humming — natural human sound is fine and often helpful — but they are NOT delivering a scripted product pitch to the lens. No "okay guys so this is..." energy. If voice is used, it's overheard, fragmented, or a single muttered line to themselves — not narration. Many of the best ideas have NO speech at all, just diegetic sound and visual storytelling.

3. **The product enters in the middle, not the opening.** First 2 seconds = the Focus hook (anomaly, intimacy, intrigue). The product reveals itself between roughly 4–10 seconds. If the product is in frame from second one, the ad reads as a commercial.

4. **One specific tribe, one specific human.** Describe the character with the precision of a casting director: age range, build, hair texture, what they're wearing in actual detail (fabric, fit, era, condition), distinguishing details (paint under fingernails, a bandaid on the thumb, sleep crease on cheek, mascara from yesterday), and most importantly their *current emotional micro-state*. The viewer should be able to picture this exact person. Demographics matter less than *tribe signals* — the clothes, the room, the way they move, the small details that say "she's one of us."

5. **Setting is a real place someone lives in, not a set.** Describe the location with sensory specificity: time of day AND quality of light (cold blue 6am light through cheap blinds, not "morning"), what's in the periphery (a half-finished crossword, a dog bed with a chewed corner, three open tabs of supplements on the counter), textures (chipped tile, pilled blanket, a coffee ring on a stack of bills), and ambient sound (a kettle, traffic, a dog's nails on hardwood).

6. **The hook is a strange specific, not random weirdness.** The opening anomaly must be earned by the product's emotional territory. If the product is a sleep supplement, the hook lives in the world of insomnia. If it's a protein bar, the hook lives in the world of post-workout depletion or the 3pm crash. The novelty creates focus *because* it's already adjacent to the product's truth.

7. **Single paragraph output.** 180–260 words. Not a script. Not bullets. No headers. A flowing paragraph with embedded timestamps that reads like a creative director walking someone through what's on screen.

9. **NO on-screen text, NO subtitles, NO captions, NO overlays — anywhere in the video.** This is a hard rule. Do NOT describe text appearing on screen at any point. No lowercase TikTok-style captions, no big bold text hooks, no subtitles, no overlay graphics, no UI elements, no popups, no logos appearing as text, no scribbled annotations, no "POV:" or "me when" overlays. The video must be entirely visual + diegetic sound. The reasons: (a) AI video generators render text terribly — it comes out garbled and instantly breaks the organic illusion, (b) text overlays read as "produced content" even when subtle, killing the UGC feel. The character can say a single short line aloud if needed (a blurt, a mutter, an "oh god") but no text appears on screen, ever.

10. **NO turned-on screens visible in the shot — anywhere.** No phones with the screen on, no laptops with the screen on, no TVs that are on, no tablets that are on, no smartwatches with an active face, no monitors playing anything, no digital clocks showing the time, no microwave displays lit up, no car dashboards lit. AI video generators render screen content as nonsensical glitch-text that destroys realism in one frame. Screens may be PRESENT in the scene but must be OFF / BLACK / DARK — a dark phone face-down on the counter, a closed laptop, a TV that's off (reflecting the room), a dark microwave. If a screen would normally be on in the scene, either turn it off, hide it, or use a non-screen alternative (e.g., a paper callback sheet instead of a phone screen, a paperback book instead of an e-reader, a wristwatch with hands instead of digital).

11. **End on emotion. The final beat is a feeling, not a CTA.** The last 2–4 seconds are the emotional landing — a small private smile, an exhale, a slowed breath, sitting back, closing eyes. No "shop now," no "link in bio," no brand tagline, no slogan, no hashtags. The product's name may appear in the paragraph for the AI generator's reference, but no marketing-voice line ever closes the ad. The ending is a feeling, full stop.

12. **Over-specify physical logic to prevent AI hallucination.** AI video generators (Veo, Sora, Runway, Seedance, Kling) hallucinate aggressively when prompts have any ambiguity about objects, hands, motion, or sequence. The paragraph must read slightly more clinically than feels artistic — every beat must specify:
    - **Which hand holds what** at every transition (left hand, right hand)
    - **Where each object was** before it appears in someone's hand (top shelf of fridge / nightstand / counter / tote bag)
    - **How objects move from A to B** (she sets down / picks up / reaches / opens)
    - **One continuous location, one continuous moment** — no flashbacks, no time jumps, no scene changes
    - **The product is used as it's actually used in real life** — a drink is drunk, a jacket is worn, a snack is eaten. Never put the product through an impossible or unintended action.
    - **If multiple branded items are in frame, the hero product must be unambiguous** — describe other items as generic and clearly separate (e.g., "an empty crinkled snack bag" rather than mixing two strong brand identities). Never imply or describe two items combining, merging, or going into each other.
    
    Default to over-description. Sounding slightly clinical is better than leaving room for the generator to invent surreal nonsense.

## Phase 0 — Read the Product

Before generating anything, study the uploaded image (and any provided description). Extract silently — never write this out in the output:

- **What it is** — category, form, packaging, materials, price tier signal
- **Who actually uses it** — not the brand's target persona, but the *real* tribe that would buy it
- **The emotional territory** — what feeling does this product address? (Anxiety, depletion, loneliness, vanity, control, comfort, ambition, exhaustion, longing, smallness, etc.)
- **The single ownable truth** — the one thing about this product that creates a real moment in someone's day. Find the moment, not the feature.
- **Sensory texture** — what it feels/smells/sounds like in use; the micro-rituals around it

If the user provided a description, weight it heavily — it tells you what THEY think matters about the product.

---

## Phase 1 — Pick the Tribe (silent T-pillar)

Before designing the scene, pick ONE specific tribe. Not "a woman in her 30s." Specific:

- "An ER nurse on her third night shift this week"
- "A divorced dad making lunches at 6:15am in a kitchen he's still learning"
- "A 22-year-old in her first studio apartment, eating ramen out of a thrifted bowl"
- "A retired contractor who started gardening to fill the hours"
- "A bride-to-be hiding from her own bachelorette party in the hotel bathroom"

The tribe choice determines everything else — the setting, the wardrobe, the energy, the hook, the closing feeling. Pick a tribe whose internal monologue *intersects* with the product's emotional territory.

---

## Phase 2 — Design the Focus Hook (silent F-pillar) — THE MAKE-OR-BREAK STEP

This is where 80% of ads fail. The hook must produce an **involuntary physical "wait WHAT"** in the first second. If you can imagine someone scrolling past it on autopilot, the hook is dead.

**Pick from these 10 viral hook patterns (use the expanded definitions in the FATE > FOCUS section above):**

1. Physical Act of Chaos (spill, drop, break, splash, smoke, mess)
2. Wrong-Body Hook (weird body position / weird action with body)
3. Extreme Close-Up Cold Open (macro mystery shot)
4. The Blurt (one uncensored line said flat)
5. Before/After in First Second (open on wreckage/aftermath/worst version)
6. Physical Transformation Mid-Frame (something changing color/state/texture)
7. The Fourth-Wall Violation (held eye contact too long)
8. The Number/Claim Slap (big lowercase confession-text on screen)
9. Physical Discomfort / Mild Body Horror (sweat, breakout, hangover face, cracked hands)
10. The Interrupted Moment (caught mid-intimate/chaotic moment)

**Rules for choosing:**

- The hook category must marry the product's emotional territory. A beauty product can do hook #5, #6, or #9. A drink can do #1, #2, or #4. A fashion item can do #2, #5, or #7. Don't force a hook that has nothing to do with the product's truth.
- **Avoid soft hooks completely.** "Pause mid-action," "looking out window," "sitting quietly" = REJECTED. If the hook involves the character being calm and contemplative in the first second, rewrite it.
- The hook must be **visually describable in one violent sentence.** "She's sitting on the bathroom floor sobbing into a hand towel" = strong. "A girl unfolds a jacket on a bed" = weak.
- **Use the strangest hook the product can earn.** Err toward more chaotic, more intimate, more body-forward, more visually loud — then dial back only if it stops feeling organic.

The first second decides everything.

---

## Phase 3 — Build the Authority + Tribe Texture (silent A + T pillars)

The middle beat (roughly 2–6s) is where the scene earns its credibility WITHOUT speaking. Load the frame with **lived-in detail** that signals "this person knows what they're doing / has been doing this a while / this is their actual life":

- The right objects in the background (not staged — *cluttered* in a way that tells a story)
- Hands that move with familiarity (not a hand model, a real hand — chipped polish, a small cut, calluses)
- A specific small ritual that only people in this tribe would do (the way they squeeze a tube, the way they hold the cup, the order they do steps in)
- Wear on the product if it's been used before, or fresh-from-packaging energy if it's new — both can sell authenticity, just pick one consciously

Authority on TikTok is *texture*, not titles. Show, don't claim.

---

## Phase 4 — The Product Moment (the shift)

Around 6–11s, the product enters or becomes the focus. Rules:

- The product must enter through the character's hand or in their natural environment — never as a clean product shot.
- **Anchor to the reference image at this exact moment.** Use one of: "the [product] from the reference image," "this exact [product] (matching the reference photo)," "the [product] pictured," "identical to the reference image — keep [print/color/label/shape] exact." Briefly describe the product's specific visible features (color, print, label, silhouette, packaging text) so the video generator gets redundant signal about what to render. This is not optional — the paragraph is paired with a product image at generation time and must instruct the generator to render *that* product.
- There should be a **micro-shift** the moment the product is used / applied / consumed / opened. Something tiny changes — their shoulders drop a quarter inch, they exhale, they pause, their face softens, they nod once to themselves. This is the proof beat. It's never overacted.
- If the product has a sensory texture (foam, click, pour, snap, peel), let the camera linger on it for ~1 second. Sensory micro-shots are pure organic-content currency.

---

## Phase 5 — The Emotion Landing (silent E-pillar)

The final 2–4 seconds are about the *feeling left behind*, not the product:

- A held look out a window
- A small unconscious smile
- An exhale
- Sitting back down
- Petting the dog
- Closing their eyes for half a second longer than normal
- A laugh under their breath

Then we hold on the final visual — a quiet, lingering image of the feeling. NO on-screen text. NO caption. NO subtitle. NO overlay of any kind. The final beat is purely cinematic.

If a closing audible cue is needed, it's a single short muttered line (3–8 words) said by the character under their breath, half to themselves — never to the camera. Like a real person letting something slip out. Examples (do not reuse, generate fresh for each product):

- "okay that actually helped"
- "fine. i'll keep buying it"
- "today wasn't so bad"
- "i think i needed that"
- "...huh"

The line is optional. Many ideas work better in pure silence with just diegetic sound (a kettle, traffic, breathing, a dog's nails on hardwood). Default to silence unless an audible line adds something specific.

NEVER include: brand taglines, "shop now," "link in bio," dramatic marketing slogans, hashtags, the product name in slogan form, any text appearing on screen.

---

## Phase 6 — Write the Pitch

Output is ONE paragraph, 180–260 words, embedded timestamps, no headers inside the paragraph itself.

**Structure (as flowing prose, not labeled):**

> *(0–2s)* [The Focus hook — present tense, sensory, drop us mid-moment. Character introduction with tribe specificity.] *(2–6s)* [The Authority + Tribe texture — what's around them, how their hands move, the lived-in details, the ambient sound.] *(6–11s)* [The product moment — how it enters with explicit reference-image anchoring, the micro-shift, the sensory close-up.] *(11–15s)* [The emotional landing — the feeling beat, the final image we hold on. Optionally one muttered line by the character in quotes — never spoken to the camera. NO on-screen text/captions/subtitles described anywhere.]

**Voice rules:**
- Present tense, cinematic, sensory
- Real timestamps embedded: *(0–2s)*, *(2–6s)*, *(6–11s)*, *(11–15s)*
- Every sentence does work — no setup, no throat-clearing
- Specific over vague at all costs. "A woman" → "A woman in her late twenties in an oversized green hoodie with a coffee stain on the sleeve, hair in a claw clip, mascara from yesterday still under one eye"
- Specific environments too: "a kitchen" → "a galley kitchen lit only by the cabinet under-lights, dishes in the sink from last night, a single ring of condensation on the counter where the wine glass was"
- No camera talk. No "we cut to" or "the camera pans." Just describe what the viewer sees and hears.
- No FATE labels. No "Focus:" or "(F:)" anywhere. The model is invisible.
- End on the emotional landing — a held visual + optionally one short muttered line in quotes spoken by the character to themselves. NO on-screen text, captions, subtitles, or overlays anywhere in the description.

## Phase 6.5 — The Logical Coherence Pass (CRITICAL — AI generators hallucinate without this)

Before writing the final paragraph, mentally **simulate the video frame-by-frame** and run it against this list. AI video generators (Veo, Sora, Runway, Seedance, Kling) hallucinate aggressively when the prompt has ambiguity about:

### 1. Object handling and continuity
- **Which hand holds what, at every beat.** If the character had Cheetos in their right hand at 0–2s and is opening a drink at 6–11s, the paragraph must specify they *set the Cheetos bag down on the floor / counter* before grabbing the drink. Don't leave it implied. Generators don't infer continuity — they invent it.
- **Where every object is before it's used.** Don't have a product "appear" in someone's hand. Specify where it was (on the top shelf of the fridge / on the nightstand / in the tote bag) and the motion that brings it into their hand.
- **What happens to objects when they leave the action.** If they take a sip and put the cup down, say where. If they throw something away, say into what. Generators will literally make objects float or merge if you don't.

### 2. Spatial logic
- **The room must make sense.** If they're "on the kitchen floor" and "reaching up to the fridge," that's coherent. If they're "on the floor" and also "leaning against the bathroom counter" two beats later, the generator will warp the space. Stay in one consistent location unless you explicitly describe the transition.
- **Distances must be plausible.** A person can't go from a balcony to a bathroom in one beat. If they move, describe the movement.
- **Camera position must stay coherent.** Don't write descriptions that imply impossible camera angles (e.g., extreme close-up of the face AND simultaneously seeing the whole room behind them — generators will create distorted fish-eye horror).

### 3. Temporal logic
- **One time of day, one continuous moment.** No flashbacks, no time jumps, no "earlier" or "later." The whole 15 seconds is ONE continuous scene happening in real time.
- **State changes need a cause.** A spilled drink stays spilled unless someone cleans it. A wet face stays wet unless wiped. Wreckage in the first frame doesn't magically resolve.
- **Cause precedes effect.** If they wince, something hot/spicy/cold happened first — say what. If they smile, something good happened first — anchor it.

### 4. Body and physics
- **Bodies do what bodies do.** People can't levitate, contort impossibly, or do two physical actions simultaneously with the same body part. Don't write "she takes a sip while wiping her eyes" if the same hand is doing both.
- **Liquid behaves like liquid.** It pours, it splashes, it pools. It doesn't hang in the air. If you describe a liquid moment, make sure gravity is in the description.
- **Clothing stays on / stays consistent.** Don't change what they're wearing mid-scene. Don't have a sleeve that's rolled up in beat 1 and rolled down in beat 3 without a moment of action.

### 5. Product logic
- **The product's function must match what's happening in the scene.** A taro milk drink is *drunk*, not eaten or wiped on the face. A bomber jacket is *worn*, not folded into a bag mid-scene. If the character interacts with the product, the interaction must be the product's actual intended use. (No exceptions — generators handle implausible product use by inventing surreal frames.)
- **The product enters via a clear, plausible motion.** "She grabs the [product] off the top shelf and pulls it down" is clear. "The [product] is there" is ambiguous — the generator will hallucinate how it got there.
- **Don't combine products in nonsensical ways.** If a Cheetos bag is on screen AND a taro drink is on screen, the paragraph must make clear they are *separate items* the character interacts with separately — never imply one goes into or comes out of the other.

### 6. Cultural / contextual logic
- **The tribe and the scene must agree.** Don't put an "exhausted ER nurse" in a freshly-cleaned designer kitchen with full makeup. Don't put a "broke college student" in a luxury apartment with marble counters. Match the visual signals to the tribe.
- **Brands and products in frame must not conflict.** If the focal product is a high-end skincare item, don't put a competitor's logo in frame. If you mention another product on screen (like the Cheetos bag in the example), make sure it's a *common, generic-enough item* that the generator won't get confused about which is the "hero" product.

### THE TEST: Read the paragraph and ask, for every beat:
- *Where exactly are her hands?*
- *Where is each object physically?*
- *What just happened to make this moment possible?*
- *Could two readers visualize this differently?* (If yes, add specificity.)
- *Could a generator confuse Product A with Product B in any way?* (If yes, clarify which is the hero and what role the other plays.)

If anything in the paragraph could be visualized two different ways, the generator will pick the worse one. **Over-specify. Be redundant. Describe the obvious.** It's better to sound slightly clinical than to leave room for hallucination.

## Phase 7 — Self-Check Before Delivering

Before sending, verify silently:

- [ ] Idea fits 15 seconds with timestamps embedded
- [ ] **HOOK IS VIRAL-GRADE.** First second produces involuntary "wait WHAT" — uses one of the 10 hook patterns (chaos / wrong-body / extreme close-up / blurt / wreckage / transformation / fourth-wall / claim-slap / body-horror / interrupted moment). If the hook is "pause and look thoughtfully," REJECT and rewrite.
- [ ] First 2 seconds are an anomaly/hook — NOT a product shot, NOT talking to camera
- [ ] Product enters in the middle, not the opening
- [ ] Character is one specific human from one specific tribe, richly described
- [ ] Setting feels like a real place someone lives in
- [ ] Authority is shown through texture, never stated
- [ ] There's a clear micro-shift moment when the product is used
- [ ] Ending is on emotion, not on product
- [ ] **Product is explicitly anchored to the reference image** (uses phrasing like "from the reference image," "matching the reference photo," "identical to the reference") AND specific visible features (color, print, label, shape, packaging) are briefly described so the AI video generator renders the exact product
- [ ] **PHYSICAL LOGIC LOCKED:** Which hand holds what is specified at every transition. Every object's location is described before it's used. Every motion (pick up, set down, reach, open) is explicit. No floating objects, no teleporting items.
- [ ] **ONE continuous location, ONE continuous moment.** No flashbacks, no time jumps, no scene changes. The whole 15s happens in real time in one spot.
- [ ] **Product is used as intended.** Drink is drunk, snack is eaten, jacket is worn. No surreal or impossible product interactions.
- [ ] **If multiple branded/identifiable items are in frame**, the hero product is unambiguously distinct. Other items are described as generic and clearly separate. NEVER imply two items combining or merging.
- [ ] **Read the paragraph and visualize it frame by frame.** Could two people picture this differently? If yes, add more specificity. Could a generator confuse which is the hero product? If yes, clarify.
